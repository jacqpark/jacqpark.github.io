Jihye Park  ·  Nicolas Bau  ·  Simone Dietrich
Department of Political Science and International Relations, University of Geneva
The question

Development finance runs through firms we do not study

  • DFIs move tens of billions a year to private firms in frontier markets OECD 2016
  • In the US, OPIC from 1971 and the DFC since the BUILD Act of 2018. One continuous instrument Lawson & Nelson 2016
  • Scholarship covers state motives, governance, allocation. The sponsors stay in the background Bau 2026; Bau, Dietrich, Qian & Trinh 2026; Dietrich 2021
  • Approval is not the end. Statutory authority, risk coverage, and diplomacy hold their value only under political conditions
The puzzle

A US federal agency creates the relationship. Why is sponsor political activity organized around particular foreign countries instead?

Political maintenance

Sustained political activity aimed at preserving and activating the public commitments on which long‑duration frontier investments depend.

Theory

A commitment attaches the US government to one firm’s project in one host country.

Tariffs, local content rules, tax regimes, currency restrictions, contract renegotiation. Host‑country risks, answered by country‑organized US instruments.

Jensen 2003; Lawson & Nelson 2016

Why the country is the unit

Backing is redeemed one country at a time

Country‑defined instruments
  • Risk insurance claims arise from a named sovereign Lawson & Nelson 2016
  • Embassy and State engagement is bilateral
  • Trade agreements, sanctions, country legislation Carcelli 2023; Broz & Hawes 2006
Not our claim
  • Multinationals watch politics wherever they hold assets Hansen & Mitchell 2000; Jensen 2003
  • DFC adds a public dimension, raising the value of access on that country
Hypothesis 1 · Project maintenance

A sponsor firm’s political engagement on a host country is higher where it holds a DFC‑backed project than where it does not.

Data and design

Panel, outcomes, estimators

2,833
OPIC and DFC projects, 2000 to 2024
15
infrastructure sponsors matched to a lobbying client
25
DFC host countries
37
firm‑country cells with an award
Maintenance
  • Outcome. Count of a firm’s LDA reports in a year whose issue text names the country. Free‑text match, LobbyView Kim 2018
  • Treatment. 1 from the year of the firm’s first OPIC or DFC project in that country onward
  • Estimator. Fixed‑effects Poisson. Count outcome with heavy zeros, treatment switches on and off, firm‑year and country or country‑year FE absorbed. Coefficients read as rate ratios
Entry
  • Outcome. Onset of a first award in a firm‑country‑year. Cells leave the risk set once it happens
  • Treatment. Any prior lobbying on that country, and the count of reports naming it over the preceding 3 years
  • Estimator. Discrete‑time logit hazard on a wider risk set of 4,001 infrastructure and energy filers. Tests whether access precedes the award

What this rules in, and out

DFC‑backed projects sit inside political relationships that precede the award and continue after public backing is in place. Selection runs partly on prior engagement, so the panel gap is not a randomly assigned award effect.

Access as a standing asset rather than a single purchase. Hall & Deardorff 2006

Limits

What the design can and cannot carry

Bounds
  • Thin base. 15 sponsors, 37 treated cells, driven by AES, Apache, Noble Energy, GE
  • Free‑text country measure. Operations and US legislation are not separable
  • No event study. Year‑by‑year SEs exceed 2 on the log scale
  • Disclosure misses informal and diplomatic channels. A lower bound
Next
  • Private capital mobilization, once transaction‑level data exists
  • The 2025 turn to critical minerals reshuffles which countries carry salience. Advocacy should follow Nakano 2025; Runde & Bandura 2020; Runde 2021

Conclusion

The country is an organizing dimension of firm lobbying

  • Sponsors are political actors, and their politics is geographic. Heaviest where their DFC exposure sits, and the filings say so
  • The division of labor is itself a finding. Coalitions hold the vehicle, sponsors hold the exposure Bombardini & Trebbi 2012; Boehmke, Gailmard & Patty 2013
  • DFIs are state strategy and private platform at once. Project durability rests on the sponsor’s standing in one bilateral relationship Malik & Stone 2018; Stone 2026; Brooks & Voeten 2026; Heldt & Meunier 2026
  • Measurement follows. Agency‑ and program‑level designs miss the part organized by geography Hansen & Mitchell 2000; Hall & Deardorff 2006

Appendix

Measurement choices, estimator choices, and what each one buys.

Universe and outcome construction, fixed‑effects Poisson, discrete‑time logit.

Park, Bau & Dietrich · Sponsor Firm Politics
Appendix A · Universe

Why infrastructure sponsors

The asset
  • Immobile, capital‑intensive, long‑lived. The sponsor cannot walk the project across a border when conditions turn Dafe & Upadhyaya 2024; Kilby 2013
  • Revenue runs through licences, tariffs, and convertibility, so its value depends on host‑country policy long after financial close Jensen 2003
The measurement
  • Infrastructure identifies a corporate sponsor by name. Funds, financial intermediaries, and on‑lending facilities do not, so there is nobody to match to a lobbying client
  • A named sponsor is what makes the firm‑country cell the unit of observation at all
  • The restriction is what produces a thin base of 15 sponsors and 37 treated cells
Park, Bau & Dietrich · Sponsor Firm Politics
Appendix B · Columns 1 and 2

Fixed‑effects Poisson, and what the two columns compare

Why Poisson
  • The outcome is a count of reports with a large mass of zeros. A log‑linear model would have to invent a transformation for them
  • Consistent under any conditional mean, so no distributional claim about the count is needed. SEs clustered by firm
  • Treatment switches on within a firm‑country cell and stays on, and the estimator absorbs high‑dimensional FE without an incidental parameters problem
  • Coefficients exponentiate into rate ratios, which is the quantity we want
The two comparisons
  • Col 1. Firm‑year and country FE. AES in El Salvador against AES in its other countries that same year, net of anything fixed about El Salvador
  • Col 2. Country‑year FE replace country FE. A triple difference, also netting out what every firm does on that country that year
  • No never‑treated control firms. The 15 sponsors supply both treated and untreated cells, which is why a small treated set is workable
  • Col 2 costs sample. 560 to 215 cells survive the fixed effects
Park, Bau & Dietrich · Sponsor Firm Politics
Appendix C · Columns 3 and 4

Discrete‑time logit and the risk set

Why a hazard model
  • The quantity is the onset of an award in a firm‑country‑year among cells still at risk, and awards arrive in annual data, so discrete time is the natural form
  • Year FE carry the baseline hazard, country FE absorb which places DFC works in, firm FE in col 4 hold constant how much a firm lobbies overall
  • Conditional logit drops FE groups with no onset, so 3,176 of 6,552 cells estimate. All 35 onsets are retained
Building the risk set
  • 15 sponsors are not a risk set. It needs firms that plausibly could have received an award and did not
  • Every LobbyView client flagged by sector keyword or by NAICS 21, 22, 23, 48, 49, 51
  • Intermediaries dropped. Coalitions, associations, chambers, institutes, universities, government entities file on behalf of others
  • 4,001 firms with at least 1 report, 2000 to 2021. Each enters in the year of its first filing
