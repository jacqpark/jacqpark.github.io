Lijphart 2012 · Two families

Winner takes all, or seats follow votes?

Plurality and majority

Winner takes all

The candidate with the most votes wins the district, and everyone who voted for someone else goes unrepresented. The party that leads nationally tends to win more than its share of seats.

Proportional representation

Seats follow votes

Majorities and minorities both win seats, and each party's share of the seats should match its share of the votes.

PR is "very difficult to defend" and even "anti-democratic."

Bill Clinton in 1993, on why he withdrew Lani Guinier's nomination. Countries often change rules within a family and rarely switch between the two.

Eleven of the 36 democracies used plurality between 1945 and 2010. The big changes all came in the 1990s, in New Zealand, Italy and Japan.

A legal threshold writes the barrier into law, and a small district builds it into the arithmetic. Lijphart's legal thresholds run from 0.67 percent in the Netherlands to 5 percent in Germany and New Zealand.

Lijphart · Four more attributes

What else shapes the result?

Assembly size

Small chambers distort more. Assemblies tend to have about the cube root of the population in seats, so Barbados "should" have 63 members and has 30.

Presidential elections

Only large parties can win the presidency, and their edge carries into legislative races. Duverger counted a dozen parties in France's Third Republic and four in the Fifth.

Malapportionment

Districts with unequal populations, usually in favor of rural areas, as in the United States before the 1960s, Japan under SNTV and Spain.

Links between parties

Linked lists pool their votes in Switzerland, Israel and the Netherlands. Under the alternative vote and STV, parties trade lower preferences.

Lijphart · Measuring disproportionality

How far do seats stray from votes?

G = √( ½ Σ (vi − si)² )

  1. Subtract each party's seat share from its vote share.
  2. Square the gaps, add them up and halve the total.
  3. Take the square root. Squaring makes one large gap count for more than several small ones.

Lijphart · Duverger and Rae

Why plurality shrinks the party system

Mechanical effect

Every party except the two strongest loses in most districts and ends up underrepresented, as the British Liberals did for decades after the war.

Psychological effect

"The electors soon realize that their votes are wasted if they continue to give them to the third party." Politicians draw the same conclusion and join a large party.

Rae · what every electoral system tends to do

Distort the voteCut the number of partiesManufacture majorities

Canada's Conservatives won a clear majority of seats with 39.6 percent of the vote in 2011. All three tendencies are much stronger under plurality and majority rules.

Electoral systems are "a crucial determinant, though by no means the sole determinant, of party systems." How divided a society is matters too.

Carey and Hix 2011 · The trade-off

Can you have both?

  1. Representation. PR mirrors the electorate. Lijphart once called proportionality "virtually synonymous with electoral justice."
  2. Accountability. Coalitions blur who is responsible, find it harder to change policy and spend more. Voters reward and punish best when few parties govern.
  3. The literature implies a straight line, where each gain on one ideal costs an equal loss on the other. Carey and Hix expect the frontier to bow outward.

"You cannot have both, so the mantra goes." The question is how much of one ideal a little of the other costs.

Carey and Hix · Why the curve bends

Three reasons for diminishing returns

Arithmetic

Going from one seat to about six per district lets parties with around 10 percent win. Most votes go to parties at least that big, so even larger districts add little.

Vote share needed for a seat under PR · T ≈ 75% ÷ (M + 1)

Strategy

Voters abandon hopeless parties when they can tell which ones are hopeless. Above about ten seats per district, almost nobody should vote strategically.

Cognition

People sort a handful of options along one dimension well, and their capacity drops sharply at seven or more (Miller 1956).

In districts of two to six seats, voters face choices much like a single-member race. Small districts should win most of PR's representation and keep most of the pressure to coordinate.

Carey and Hix · What the evidence shows

Who chose the rules?

  1. The scatter in Figure 2 is "just descriptive," pooled across countries with no controls.
  2. Rules are institutional choices. Most of Latin America uses PR and most former British colonies use plurality, and the number of parties when the franchise widened is widely seen as shaping the choice.
  3. Carey and Hix argue that technical details such as magnitude are harder to predict. Their fixed-effects models rely on reforms within countries, which are rare.

Session 05 asked the same question about constitutions. When countries choose their institutions, comparing outcomes mixes the rule with the place.
