Katz and Mair 1995 · The argument

Decline, or a new kind of party?

The decline literature

Parties judged by their roots

Since Ostrogorski, parties have been classified by their ties to civil society, with the mass party as the standard. Falling membership and weaker loyalties then read as decline.

Katz and Mair

Parties judged by their ties to the state too

Each party type provokes a reaction that produces the next. The mass party was tied to a conception of democracy and an ideal of social structure that no longer fit postindustrial societies.

"The mass party is simply one stage in a continuing process."

They treat party development as a dialectic with no end point. The newest stage grows out of an ever closer symbiosis between parties and the state.

Katz and Mair · Parties, civil society and the state

From civil society to the state

19th century

Elite party

Committees of the few who ran both the state and civil society. Trustees.

1880 to 1960

Mass party

Rooted in civil society and bridging to the state. Delegates of one segment.

1945 onward

Catch-all party

Brokers standing between civil society and the state. Entrepreneurs.

1970 onward

Cartel party

Absorbed by the state and living on its resources. Agents of the state.

"Parties have now become semi-state agencies."

The first three panels follow the article's Figures 1 to 3. The fourth draws Table 1's "party becomes part of state." Each type grew out of a reaction to the one before.

"Elections became choices of delegates rather than trustees."

Duverger expected a "contagion from the left" that would force every party to copy the mass party. The old parties copied the form, with members, branches and a congress, and kept the parliamentary party in charge.

Katz and Mair · The catch-all party

A victim of its own success

  1. The big battles for political and social rights were won, and the solidarity they had built faded.
  2. The welfare state took over services that the party and its network used to provide.
  3. Better living conditions, mobility and mass media blurred once distinct constituencies.
  4. Leaders who had tasted office wanted to keep winning and reached beyond their original base.
What changed
  • People join because they agree with the policies, and membership becomes one affiliation among many.
  • Television lets leaders appeal to every voter directly.
  • In government the party is "Janus-like." It carries demands to the bureaucracy and defends the bureaucracy's policies to the public.

The party trades "effectiveness in depth for a wider audience and more immediate electoral success."

Kirchheimer's phrase. Unions now deal with whichever party governs. In the pluralist view that grew up alongside it, party oligarchy "becomes a virtue rather than a vice."

Katz and Mair · Parties and the state

The state pays

Members fall short▶Costs climb▶Parties vote subsidies▶Subsidies follow past results▶Newcomers shut out
The broker's commission

Brokers have interests of their own. A party that can manipulate the state for its clients can also manipulate it for itself.

The rules

Parties in government set both the rules for state funding and the amounts. Access to state-regulated broadcasting works the same way.

The state "becomes an institutionalized structure of support, sustaining insiders while excluding outsiders."

The risk is dependence, since losing office could now cost a party its income. Yet all parties can survive together, and that makes the conditions ideal for a cartel.

Katz and Mair · The cartel party

Nobody is ever out

  1. Almost all substantial parties now count as governing parties, especially once regional and local government is included.
  2. Opposition rarely cuts a party off from state spoils, patronage, media or subsidies. Ireland and the UK even give opposition parties more subsidy.
  3. The cartel party combines "the interpenetration of party and state" with "a pattern of inter-party collusion."

Katz and Mair · Contained competition

Rivals who share

Italy

The major parties shared patronage, sometimes with the Communists, who were ostensibly in opposition.

Switzerland

The four main parties share the seats of the Federal Council and rotate its presidency.

Netherlands

The parties share appointments of mayors.

United States

Redistricting in many states draws "incumbent protection" gerrymanders.

Parties compete knowing they share "a mutual interest in collective organizational survival."

Sometimes there is even "a positive incentive not to compete." Campaigns become capital intensive, professional and centralized, and state money pays for more of them.

Katz and Mair · Members and leaders

Members as individuals

More rights for members▶One member, one postal ballot▶Activists bypassed▶Leaders gain autonomy
Inside the party

The line between members and supporters blurs. Leaders point to a large, formally empowered membership to legitimize their position.

Stratarchy

The center leaves local parties a free hand on local matters. Local leaders stay out of national affairs, because the center can appeal to members directly.

Picture a party "that manages all of its business from a single central headquarters, and which simply subdivides its mailing list."

In the mass party, members held the elite to account from the bottom up. Cartel party members are "valued for contribution to legitimizing myth."

Democracy becomes "a service provided by the state for civil society."

No major party is ever definitively out, which weakens voters' power to punish anyone. "Stability becomes more important than triumph; politics becomes a job rather than a vocation."

Katz and Mair · Challenges to the cartel

The cartel breeds its challengers

  1. Parties stop carrying demands from civil society. Interest organizations take over, and neocorporatism brings unions and employers inside the system.
  2. Demands the coopted groups will not voice feed new organizations, often short lived and strident.
  3. Barriers to newcomers, such as subsidies tied to past results or limits on ballot access, can hand the excluded a weapon.
Absorbed

D66 in the Netherlands, the Liberal Democrats in the UK, the Progressive Democrats in Ireland and many Green parties. Most proved willing to join the establishment they had attacked.

Radical right

Vlaams Blok, the French National Front and the Austrian Freedom Party among others. They gain mileage from attacking the "cosy" deals among established parties.

"These new protesters do not represent a challenge to party; their protest is, after all, organized by party."

Perot in the United States and the Reform Party in Canada ran on a demand to "break the mould." Katz and Mair conclude that the challenge falls on cartel parties in particular.
