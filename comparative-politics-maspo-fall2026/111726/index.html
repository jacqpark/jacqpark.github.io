Ordinary least squares

What a regression does

Y = a + bX + errorThe points are invented

  1. OLS draws the straight line that makes the squared vertical gaps between points and line, the residuals, as small as possible.
  2. The slope is the coefficient. Here one more unit of X goes with about .52 more units of Y. The intercept is the constant, the predicted Y where X is zero.
  3. With several predictors, OLS fits all the slopes at once. Each coefficient is then the slope of its own X with the other predictors held constant.

Hanson and Sigman's and Carey and Hix's tables come from OLS. Every coefficient in them is a slope of this kind.

Coefficients

Say it as a sentence

One more unit of X goes with β more units of Y, the other predictors held constant

Hanson and Sigman · −12.15

One more point of Capacity in 1960, about one standard deviation, goes with about 12 fewer infant deaths per 1,000 births in 2010.

Carey and Hix · .01

"A jump from a median magnitude of 1 to 10 is expected to boost the fragmentation index by one-tenth of a unit."

Gilens and Page · .76

The predictors are transformed and squeezed into a 0 to 1 range. The authors call the magnitudes "difficult to interpret" and report predicted probabilities instead.

Check the units on both sides before you translate. When the units are opaque, look for predicted values.

Standard errors

One sample, one estimate

Invented sampling distributions of one coefficient

  1. A table reports the slope from one sample. Another sample of countries or years would give a somewhat different slope.
  2. Repeat the draw many times and the estimates form a curve around the true value. The standard error is the typical distance from its center.
  3. More cases and less scatter around the line narrow the curve. Four times the cases halves the standard error.

Carey and Hix's magnitude coefficient has a standard error of .004 across countries and .03 within them. Fewer useful comparisons make a wider curve.

Hypothesis tests

Is the coefficient far from zero?

Hanson and Sigman, Table 5, column 1

  1. Start from the null hypothesis that the true coefficient is zero.
  2. Divide the coefficient by its standard error. The result, t, counts how many standard errors the estimate sits from zero.
  3. The p-value is the chance of a t at least this far out if the null were true. Below .05, the null is rejected and the table adds a star.

Capacity sits 3.88 standard errors from zero, Democracy only 1.27 (p ≈ .2). The p-value says how surprising the estimate would be under the null.

Two ways to be wrong

Type I and Type II errors

No real effectA real effect
Star, null rejectedType I error · false positiveCorrect
No starCorrectType II error · false negative
Type I

The cut-off, called α, sets its rate. At .05, one test in twenty of a true null earns a star by chance. Hanson and Sigman's Table 5 runs 24 tests.

Type II

Its rate, called β, rises with smaller effects, noisier data and fewer cases. Power, 1 − β, is the chance of catching an effect that is there.

The trade-off

A stricter cut-off such as .01 buys fewer false positives with more false negatives. With 1,779 cases, Gilens and Page have the power to star a correlation of .12.

Democracy's −9.40 across 148 countries could be a real effect the test was too weak to detect. A missing star is weak evidence when power is low.

Confidence intervals

What 95 percent means

20 invented samples, one interval each

  1. Go 1.96 standard errors either side of the coefficient. Two is close enough for reading in your head.
  2. The 95 percent describes the method. Across repeated samples, 19 of every 20 intervals built this way contain the true coefficient.
  3. Interval and test agree. A 95 percent interval that excludes zero means p < .05 for that coefficient.

Hanson and Sigman's Capacity runs from about −18 to −6 deaths per 1,000. An interval shows the size and the precision of an effect at once.

Gilens and Page · Table 3

Reading intervals side by side

Gilens and Page, Table 3, Model 4 · coefficient ± 2 standard errors

  1. Elites' interval sits far from zero. Their influence is large and precisely estimated.
  2. Citizens' interval includes zero, so no star. It also stops at .19, well short of the elites' .60.
  3. The intervals for groups and elites overlap. Ranking the two would need a direct test of their difference, which the table does not report.

Gilens and Page call the citizens' estimate "non-significant, near-zero." The interval shows how near.

Control variables

What holding constant does

Gilens and Page, Tables 2 and 3

  1. Model 1 asks whether policies citizens favor pass more often. Model 4 asks whether citizens' views still matter among policies that the affluent and the groups rate the same.
  2. Leaving out a variable that shapes both the predictor and the outcome biases the coefficient. Model 1 credits citizens with the pull of the affluent, who agree with them and drive adoption.
  3. A control that is itself a result of the predictor soaks up part of its effect. Ask when each control was measured.

Hanson and Sigman call their controls for wealth, democracy and taxes "a very challenging test." Ask what each control takes away.

Interaction terms

When slopes differ by group

Y = a + b₁X + b₂D + b₃X×DInvented example · Y = 3 + .3X − 2D + .6X×D

  1. Without the product term, OLS gives both groups the same slope and the lines run parallel.
  2. With it, b₁ is the slope of X where D = 0, and b₁ + b₃ is the slope where D = 1.
  3. b₂ is the gap between the groups at X = 0. Here D = 1 starts 2 lower and ends higher, so b₂ alone misleads.

D could be a regime type and X a level of party fragmentation. Read b₁ and b₃ together.

Fixed effects

Between countries or within them

Invented data · three countries, four elections each

  1. A pooled regression compares every case with every other, so differences between countries drive the slope.
  2. Country fixed effects give each country its own intercept. The slope then comes only from changes inside each country.
  3. Every stable trait of a country, measured or not, drops out. The price is that only countries that change contribute.

Here the two slopes point in opposite directions. Fixed effects keep only the solid lines.

Take-away

Six questions for any table

  1. What is the outcome, and in what units?
  2. What does one unit of each predictor mean?
  3. How wide is the interval, and does it include zero?
  1. Could a star be a false positive, or a missing star a false negative?
  2. What is held constant, and was any control measured after the predictor?
  3. Does the comparison run across cases, within them, or only under some condition?
