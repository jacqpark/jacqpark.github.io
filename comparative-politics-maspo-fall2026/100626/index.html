Acemoglu and Robinson 2006 · Chapter 2

Four paths

Britain

Consolidated democracy

Reform acts from 1832 widened the vote in steps, with no significant reversal.

Argentina

Unconsolidated democracy

Democracy became real with the 1912 electoral reforms. Coups returned the country to dictatorship more than once.

Singapore

Nondemocracy, little force

The regime lasts with minor concessions and without significant repression.

South Africa

Nondemocracy by repression

Apartheid survived on repression and violence until the regime was forced to democratize in 1994.

"Why are some countries democratic whereas others are ruled by dictatorships or other nondemocratic regimes?"

Acemoglu and Robinson · The approach

Three building blocks

01

Interests

People rank regimes by what they deliver. In 1839 the Chartist J. R. Stephens called universal suffrage "a knife and fork question."

02

Conflict

Every policy creates winners and losers. The elite may be the rich, a racial or ethnic group, or the military. The citizens are more numerous.

03

Institutions

Political institutions allocate power today and tomorrow. Changing them is a way to commit to future policy.

The models treat regimes as a dichotomy, with free and fair elections and free entry into politics at the core. Compare that with the four attributes Munck and Verkuilen asked for last week.

Acemoglu and Robinson · Political power

Two kinds of power

De jure

Power that political institutions allocate. In the authors' example, Labour decides policy in Britain because voters put it in office.

De facto

What a group can do by force. Excluded citizens are the majority, and they can riot, strike or threaten revolution.

Transitory

A real threat needs the masses to solve their collective-action problem and build momentum, and it needs a military that fails to stop them. Such moments pass.

Actual power combines the two. Institutions last, while the citizens' de facto power comes and goes.

Acemoglu and Robinson · Commitment

Why not just promise?

Threat of revolution today▶Elite promises redistribution▶Threat fades▶Promise is dropped
The problem

Citizens know the elite would honor the promise only while it fears them. A promise nobody believes will not stop a revolution.

The fix

Put future decisions in the hands of the people who want the policy. Democratization moves de jure power to the majority and makes the commitment credible.

"The principle of my reform is, to prevent the necessity of revolution . . . reforming to preserve and not to overthrow."

Earl Grey presenting his electoral reform to Parliament in 1831.

Acemoglu and Robinson · The elite's options

Concede, democratize or repress

Concede

Redistribute today and promise more tomorrow. Cheap for the elite, and useless once the promise loses credibility.

Democratize

Hand over de jure power. The new institutions persist because parties and unions invest in them, and the majority gains some say over the military.

Repress

Keep power with no concessions. Lives and assets are destroyed, sanctions may follow as they did for South Africa in the 1980s, and failed repression can bring revolution.

Democracy arises "when concessions are not credible and repression is not attractive because it is too costly."

Acemoglu and Robinson · Consolidation

Coups run the same logic backward

DemocratizationCoup
Who holds power for nowCitizens, in a moment of unrestThe elite, allied with the military in a crisis
Whose promise is doubtedThe elite's promise to redistributeThe citizens' promise to moderate
What locks it inDemocracyNondemocracy
ExampleArgentina 1912, after unrest led by the Radical PartyChile 1973, during an oil shock and a deep depression

A coup turns the elite's passing de facto power into lasting de jure power. The more democracy redistributes, the more a coup is worth to the elite.

Acemoglu and Robinson · Figures 2.1 and 2.3

Democracy in a picture

  1. Region A. Inequality is low, so the poor have no reason to revolt. Singapore stays a nondemocracy with little repression.
  2. Region B. Revolution is a threat and repression costs too much, so the elite concedes. Britain and Argentina.
  3. Region C. Democracy would cost the elite so much that repression is worth it. South Africa until 1994.
  4. Inequality fell, while industry and globalization pushed the boundary down to the pink line. South Africa crossed into Region B between 1970 and 1994.

Transitions are likeliest at middle levels of inequality. Figure 2.2 adds that higher inequality makes coups pay, which separates Britain from Argentina.

Acemoglu and Robinson · Comparative statics

What shifts the odds

Civil society

An organized majority makes the revolutionary threat real and coups harder to pull off.

Crises

Depressions, debt crises and wars hand temporary power to both sides. Argentina democratized after the Falklands War, and Allende fell during the 1973 oil shock.

Land or capital

Land is easy to tax and its owners lose less from turmoil. Landed elites repress and back coups. Industrial elites concede.

Middle class

A large, affluent middle class keeps redistribution modest. Compare Costa Rica and Colombia with Guatemala, El Salvador and Nicaragua.

Globalization

Mobile capital is hard to tax, so democracy frightens elites less. Trade helps or hurts depending on what a country has in abundance.

Identities

Conflict can run along race or ethnicity, as in South Africa or Mauritius. Many cross-cutting cleavages keep redistribution low and help consolidation.

Each factor changes what democracy would cost the elite, or what repression and coups would cost it.

Acemoglu and Robinson · Institutions

Guarantees for the old elite

  1. Pinochet's constitution under-represented urban areas and kept a military veto. On this account it eased Chile's transition and produced a less redistributive, more stable democracy.
  2. South Africa's interim constitution gave any party with twenty or more seats cabinet posts in proportion to its seats. The ANC accepted limits on its own power to get the transition.
  3. Colombia's two traditional parties used the design of proportional representation to keep the left out. Democracy consolidated, amid complaints that it ignores the majority.
Haggard and Kaufman 1995

"Ironically, the greater security for the armed forces during the initial years of the transition probably reduced the threat to civilian authority in Chile, Turkey, and Korea."

The limit
Give the elite too much power and democracy does little for the majority. Revolution or repression returns.

A transition deal has to protect the old elite enough to prevent a coup and leave the majority enough to prevent a revolt.

Lührmann and Lindberg 2019

Why "autocratization"?

Three problems with "democratic backsliding"

Democratic

An autocracy cannot suffer democratic backsliding into a deeper dictatorship. Stretching the term that far is what Sartori warned against.

Back

The word implies a return to an earlier state. Regimes can also move in a new direction, toward a different form of authoritarianism.

Sliding

Sliding sounds involuntary and unconscious. Rulers who dismantle democracy act on purpose.

Autocratization is a "substantial de-facto decline of core institutional requirements for electoral democracy."

Lührmann and Lindberg · Figure 1

One umbrella, three kinds

Recession

A democracy loses democratic traits and stays a democracy, so far. Hungary under Orbán.

Breakdown

A democracy becomes an autocracy. Fujimori suspended Peru's constitution and parliament in 1992.

Consolidation

An autocracy gets worse. Al-Bashir's 1989 coup turned Sudan's electoral autocracy into a closed dictatorship.

Lührmann and Lindberg · Measurement

Coding an episode

  1. A potential episode starts when the Electoral Democracy Index falls by 0.01 or more in one year.
  2. It continues while the index keeps falling, through up to four years with no further fall.
  3. It ends after four years with no fall, or as soon as the index rises by 0.02 or more in a year.
  4. It counts as an episode if the total fall, measured from the year before it started, reaches 0.10.
The data
Index
V-Dem's EDI, from 0 to 1, built on Dahl's polyarchy
Coverage
182 countries from 1900 to 2017, or 18,031 country-years
Example
Hungary 2006 to 2017, a fall of 0.11

The authors call the 0.10 cut-off "naturally arbitrary." What would Munck and Verkuilen ask about it?

Lührmann and Lindberg · 1900 to 2017

A century of episodes

217

Autocratization episodes, in 109 countries.

65%

Began in countries that were already autocracies, 142 episodes in all.

75

Began in democracies.

80%

Of those 75 ended with the country an autocracy.

69 countries had no episode. 33 of them are autocracies with little left to lose, and 36 are democracies such as Sweden, Switzerland, Bhutan and Namibia.

Lührmann and Lindberg · Figure 2

Three waves of autocratization

First wave

32 episodes, in democracies and autocracies alike.

Second wave

62 episodes, almost all in electoral autocracies.

Third wave

75 episodes by 2017, mostly in democracies.

In 2017 more countries were autocratizing than democratizing, for the first time since 1940. The share of democracies still stood near its peak, at 53 percent.

Lührmann and Lindberg · Figure 3

Behind a legal façade

Autogolpe

An executive who took office legally suddenly abolishes elections or parliament. Fujimori in 1992, and Hitler with the Enabling Act in 1933.

Democratic erosion

An executive who took office legally wears down democratic norms gradually and leaves the key institutions standing. Hungary and Poland.

Germany's 1930-35 episode included a one-year fall of 26 percent of the scale. Turkey from 2008 and Russia from 1993 never lost more than 7 and 5 percent in a year.

Lührmann and Lindberg · Conclusion

Alarm or calm?

Reasons for calm
  • More than half of all countries were democracies in 2017
  • Third-wave episodes end at a median EDI of 0.45, against 0.13 before
  • Open coups and stolen elections are costly at home and abroad, so would-be autocrats "play a game of deception"
Reasons for alarm
  • The wave mostly hits democracies
  • Of 33 erosion episodes, 18 ended in breakdown and 10 were still running in 2017. Only 5 stopped short.
  • Gradual decline gives citizens fewer clear moments to mobilize

"As it was premature to announce the 'end of history' in 1992, it is premature to proclaim the 'end of democracy' now."
