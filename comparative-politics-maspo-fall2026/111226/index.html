Gilens and Page 2014 · Four traditions

Who governs?

Majoritarian electoral democracy

The average citizen

Vote-seeking parties converge on the median voter, so policy follows what the average citizen wants. Downs, and what Dahl called "populistic democracy."

Economic-elite domination

The wealthy

People with high income or wealth dominate key issues despite elections. Beard, Domhoff, and Winters's "oligarchy."

Majoritarian pluralism

Groups that stand in for everyone

Struggle among many groups yields policy close to what citizens need. Madison's factions, Truman, Dahl's New Haven.

Biased pluralism

Business groups

The universe of groups is tilted, and policy leans toward corporations and business and professional associations. Schattschneider, Stigler, Lindblom.

Each tradition has a large literature and a great deal of evidence behind it. Until recently none had been tested against the others in a single model.

Gilens and Page · Pluralism and its critics

Will the missing groups form?

Truman

Potential groups

Every interest has some influence. Officials must heed the groups that would form if those interests were trampled.

Olson

Free riders

Large, dispersed interests rarely organize. Each member's stake is small, everyone lets someone else do it, and officials can ignore them.

Schattschneider

An upper-class accent

The heavenly chorus of groups sings with an "upper-class accent." Business prevails when the scope of conflict is narrow and visibility low.

Most interest groups and lobbyists represent business firms or professionals, and few represent the poor. If business wins, is it because business groups are many or because they are rich?

Gilens and Page · What the measures miss

Which way do the errors cut?

The affluent proxy

The 90th percentile is "neither very rich nor very elite." Still, the top 2 percent's preferences correlate .91 with the top 10 percent's and .69 with the average citizen's.

The groups

Only a small fraction of active groups is counted. Baumgartner's team found that the Power 25 count correlates .73 with their full index of group resources.

The policies

Only proposals a national poll asked about. They lean toward high salience and cover only a slice of all possible policies.

All three predictors together explain little of the variation in outcomes (R² = .074). Gilens and Page argue the flaws make elite and group influence look smaller than it is.

  1. Taken one at a time, each actor's preferences predict policy change. Each tradition has its adherents for good reason.
  2. Entered together, the average citizen's effect falls to near zero. Elites and groups keep almost all of theirs.
  3. Average citizens and the affluent agree often (r = .78), so a link that shows up alone can be spurious.

All predictors run from 0 to 1, and the outcome is whether the change happened. Pure majoritarian electoral democracy "can be decisively rejected."

Gilens and Page · Figure 1

What moves the odds of a change?

  1. Low support means one in five in favor, or five groups strongly opposed and none in favor. High means four in five, or five groups strongly in favor.
  2. Each line holds the other actors at neutral, half in favor and a net group score of zero.
  3. The average citizen's line stays flat at about .3. A tiny minority and a large majority get the same odds.

Roughly 15 percent of proposals fall beyond these cut-offs. The low and high points are realistic values near the edges of the data.

Gilens and Page · Average citizens

Democracy by coincidence

When citizens win

Average citizens and the affluent want the same thing much of the time (r = .78). Citizens then get the policy they want as "coincidental beneficiaries rather than causes of the victory."

When they disagree

The disagreements are important. They include trade restrictions, tax policy, corporate regulation, abortion and school prayer. On these, economic elites "generally get their way."

"When push comes to shove, actual influence matters."

Earlier work found policy matching majority opinion about two-thirds of the time, and as description that still holds. Read as causal impact, those findings "appear to be largely or wholly spurious."

Gilens and Page · Status quo bias

Eight in ten is still too few

~30%

Of changes backed by a narrow public majority that happened.

~43%

~56%

Of changes strongly backed by both the affluent and interest groups that happened.

55%

Of cases with groups engaged where most groups opposed the change. Most favored it in 36 percent.

Federalism, the separation of powers, bicameralism and congressional rules all protect the status quo. A majority that opposes a change usually gets its way, and a majority that wants one usually does not.

Baumgartner et al. · Figure 1.1

Who shows up, and who gets noticed

  1. The issue identifiers were drawn in proportion to lobbying activity, so they mirror the lobbying community. Corporations, trade and professional groups are 74 percent of them.
  2. Among the major participants on the same issues, those three fall to 48 percent and citizen groups rise from 15 to 26 percent.
  3. Citizen groups are spread thin. The American Petroleum Institute has about 270 staff and a $42 million budget for petroleum issues alone.

Unions are 6 percent of interest-group participants, and six unions account for more than half of those mentions. The identifiers, who set the agenda, are systematically wealthier.

Baumgartner et al. · Four starting points

The status quo already reflects power

01 · Existing policy

Lobbying aims to change a policy already in place, the product of earlier rounds of bargaining.

02 · Complex effects

Every policy studied touched many constituencies. Trade with China could be argued in terms of farmers, textile makers, human rights or national security.

03 · Mixed sides

The sides that defend or attack a policy are rarely homogeneous. "Lone ranger" lobbying for a single favor is far from the norm.

04 · Scarce attention

Thousands of bills and hundreds of hearings compete for space. A reasonable solution is usually not enough to get on the agenda.

"If the wealthy are so certain to win, they should already have won in a previous iteration of the process."

Baumgartner et al. · Six expectations

What the four observations imply

  1. The link between material resources and getting what you want is close to zero.
  2. Resources mobilized to change the status quo and to protect it are roughly equal in most cases.
  3. Sides are heterogeneous, with rich and poor groups on the same side.
  1. Resources matter more for whole sides than for single lobbyists, and not much for either.
  2. Policy is usually stable. When it changes, it tends to change a lot.
  3. When mobilization is lopsided the richer side wins, above all when it defends the status quo.

When the goal is change, interest groups are "a surprisingly ineffectual lot." One of the best single predictors of success is whether a side is protecting the policy already in place.

Gilens and Page built their group measure on a proxy Baumgartner's team had validated. Where exactly do they disagree?

Compare the readings

Can both be right?

Different issues

Gilens and Page study the public's agenda, proposals worth a poll question. Baumgartner's team studies the lobbying agenda, mostly issues the public never hears about.

Resources or preferences

Baumgartner's team asks whether a side's money and staff predict its success, and expects past power to be built into the status quo already. Gilens and Page ask whose stated preferences predict which changes happen.

A shared tilt

Both find a strong status quo bias, and both find mobilization skewed toward business and the professions. Baumgartner's team calls the status quo "a quite unfair equilibrium in many cases."

Baumgartner and colleagues see two ways to change the skew. The missing constituencies mobilize, or elected officials learn whose voices are muffled and whose are absent.
