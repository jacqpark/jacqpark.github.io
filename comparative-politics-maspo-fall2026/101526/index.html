"The superior historical performance of parliamentary democracies is no accident."

In 1990 the United States was the only presidential democracy with a long record of constitutional continuity. Linz expects parliaments to matter most where cleavages run deep and parties are many.

Linz 1990 · Dual legitimacy

Two mandates, one fixed clock

Chile 1970

Allende, 36.2 percent

A plurality won by a heterogeneous coalition gave him a six-year mandate to run the government.

Spain 1979

Suárez, 35.1 percent

A plurality of about the same size left him working with other parties to sustain a minority government.

"Who has the stronger claim to speak on behalf of the people: the president or the legislative majority that opposes his policies?"

No democratic principle settles that dispute, Linz argues. In the past the armed forces were often tempted to step in as a mediating power.

Linz 1990 · Rigidity

Government crisis or regime crisis?

WhenParliamentaryPresidential

"What in a parliamentary system would be a government crisis can become a full-blown regime crisis in a presidential system."

The clock also breeds urgency, what Hirschman called "the wish of vouloir conclure." A president barred from reelection rushes to build "his Brasilia."

  1. The winner takes the whole executive for a fixed term. The losers wait four or five years with no share of power.
  2. No party had a majority. A left united behind González would have needed the Communists, and a popular-front mood would have run the campaign.
  3. Suárez could hardly have united the right with Fraga's AP, which stood for continuity with Franco.
  4. In a close race, small extremist parties can claim to be the decisive bloc.

A presidential election "would have been far more divisive," Linz concludes. He adds that a centrist electorate defuses the danger, as Goldwater and McGovern learned.

Cheibub and Limongi · The majoritarian imperative

Minority governments and deadlock

  1. Minority cabinets governed parliamentary democracies 22 percent of the time from 1946 to 1999.
  2. Presidents lacked a majority in about 40 percent of presidential years, close to the third of parliamentary governments in industrialized democracies.
  3. Strom shows that minority cabinets can be a rational choice when parties can shape policy from outside government.
  4. Deadlock needs a veto the legislature cannot override. Parliaments deadlock too when government follows government and no stable majority forms.

Cheibub and Limongi · Incentives for cooperation

Presidents build coalitions too

Share of governments that are coalitions · Table 2

51% Largest party under a third Parliamentary

  1. Most prime ministers leave office without an election, 56 percent in 21 industrialized democracies from 1946 to 1995. The threat of early elections is a weak source of party discipline.
  2. The two systems differ in the fallback. If no deal is struck, a parliament faces new elections and a president keeps every portfolio.
  3. Paralysis needs unusual rules. In only 4 percent of presidential years could a president veto a bill with no override.

Coalitions grow more common as parties multiply, in both systems. That cuts against Mainwaring's fear that presidentialism and multipartism are a difficult combination.

Presidential

The president issues decrees, requests urgency and alone introduces budget and tax bills. Presidents introduced 86 percent of the bills enacted.

Committees hold the gates. The president cannot introduce bills and has only a package veto.

Tsebelis gave the agenda to the government in parliaments and to the legislature in presidential systems. His rule fits two of the four cells.

Cheibub and Limongi · Where presidents come from

The constitution or the country?

  1. Presidential democracies emerge more often from military dictatorships than from civilian ones. Cheibub and Limongi note that this makes them likelier to fall back into dictatorship.
  2. With origin held constant, presidential democracies still die sooner, as Przeworski and his coauthors found in 2000.
  3. Last week's chapter offers a mechanism. A directly elected president is likelier to be populist, threatens the elite more and may invite more coups (Acemoglu and Robinson, p. 35).

Session 01 warned about how cases are selected. When the cases that adopt an institution differ from the rest, comparing their outcomes mixes the two.

Draft A · Presidential

A president elected for six years with no reelection, and a runoff if no one wins half the vote. The assembly sits for four years. The president names the cabinet and can veto bills, and two thirds of the assembly can override.

Draft B · Parliamentary

A prime minister and cabinet who need the assembly's confidence. A ceremonial president chosen by the assembly. The prime minister may call early elections.

Cheibub and Limongi, Table 3. Presidential democracies whose largest party held under a third of the seats had an expected life of 54.9 years with a coalition government and 23.9 years without one.

  1. Make Linz's case against Draft A with these results. Who reaches the runoff, which allies does each finalist need, and what can go wrong once the winner takes office?
  2. Make Cheibub and Limongi's reply. Which facts in the file would they weigh first, and which clauses would they write into Draft A?
  3. Choose a draft as a group and name the biggest risk it leaves.
