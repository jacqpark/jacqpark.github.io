From concept to score

Conceptualization covers the first two steps, measurement the next two and aggregation the last. A mistake early in the chain carries into every later step.

Two standards

Valid, reliable, both or neither?

  1. Validity is "the extent to which the proposed measures actually measure what they are supposed to measure."
  2. Reliability is the prospect that the same data collection process would always produce the same data. Agreement among coders is the usual test.
  3. "Reliable measures need not be valid ones." In fact, "one way to obtain very reliable measures is to adopt similar biases."

The bullseye is the concept. Each dot is one attempt to score the same case.

Measurement error

Noise or bias?

Random error

Scatter in both directions

A coder misreads a source, or a survey draws an unlucky sample. The errors cancel out on average, and they make real relationships harder to see.

Systematic error

A push in one direction

The error follows some other feature of the case. Collecting more of the same data leaves it in place.

Munck and Verkuilen's examples of systematic error

Reported rapes may track changes in culture more than the number of rapes.

More evidence of corruption may reflect a freer press.

Vanhanen's turnout and largest-party share build bias into his index.

"The record left by history is inherently biased." Choose indicators less exposed to bias and check them against several sources.

When indicators drift

The number moves, the concept stays put

Across places

An indicator can mean different things in different countries, so Munck and Verkuilen ask for cross-system equivalence. Police officers per person barely track Capacity (r = .03), perhaps because weak rulers hire more of them.

Over time

After 1989 Freedom House moved from a single coder to a team coding components. A shift in the series may come from the method.

Under pressure

Once money or reputation rides on a score, governments have reasons to move the indicator itself. Relabeling contracts is quicker than cleaning them up.

Drift is hard to see in the scores themselves. Ask what else could have moved the number.

Group exercise 01 · Diagnose the score

A donor ministry asks your group for advice

The ministry wants to reward partner countries that cut corruption. Would this score show which ones did?

Hanson and Sigman · Validity checks

Four ways to check a measure

CheckQuestionHanson and Sigman's Capacity
Face and contentDo the scores look right, and do they cover the whole concept?Singapore ranks in the top 25, and Somalia, Yemen and the Central African Republic sit near the bottom
ConvergentDoes it agree with other measures of the same concept?r = .91 with the World Bank's government effectiveness index
DiscriminantDoes it stay apart from different concepts?Low correlations with conflict, population and oil output, and r = .79 with log GDP per capita
ConstructDoes it predict what theory says it should?One point more Capacity goes with about 21 points more undeliverable letters returned

They follow Adcock and Collier (2001). Each check answers a different doubt, and none of them settles every doubt.

Munck and Verkuilen · Correlation tests

Why agreement proves little

  1. Shared sources. Many democracy indices drew on the same precoded data, so a high correlation "may mean that all indices are reflecting the same bias."
  2. Reliability at most. "One can get very consistent (i.e. reliable) measurements that are not valid," Bollen warned in 1986.
  3. Aggregate scores. The two Freedom House series line up with the other indices on one dimension and load alone on a second.

"The high degree of correlation among existing democracy indices does not put to rest concerns about their validity."

Agreement counts for more when the other measure rests on a different kind of data. Hanson and Sigman's census-based Myers index is one example.

  2. Their two measures of representation correlate at only .16.

Lührmann and Lindberg called their own 0.10 cut-off "naturally arbitrary." Cut-offs are measurement choices too.

Group exercise 02 · Test the accountability measure

Thursday's reform commission wants a second opinion

Do fewer parties really mean more accountable government?

The commission plans to rank Options A, B and C on accountability with Carey and Hix's two measures, the effective number of parties and the number of parties in government. Carey and Hix justify the measures this way.

Carey and Hix, p. 388

The number of parties in the first cabinet "serves as an index of how difficult it is for voters to attribute responsibility for government performance and reward or punish governing parties at the next election accordingly."

Carey and Hix, p. 384

Empirical studies indicate "that both prospective and retrospective voting are more effective when coalitions include fewer parties."

  1. Content. What does accountability mean here, and what does a count of parties leave out? Describe a country that scores well on both measures where voters still cannot remove the government.
  2. Convergent and discriminant. Name one other measure the party counts should agree with, and one different concept they should not simply track. What does Lijphart's −0.57 do to Carey and Hix's trade-off?
  3. Construct. If fewer parties do mean more accountability, what should happen to governments after a bad term? Sketch a test and the result that would count against the measure.
Take-away

Six questions for any number

  1. What is the concept, and is it defined too broadly or too narrowly?
  2. Which indicators stand for it, and what else moves them?
  3. Is the error random or systematic?
  1. Does the indicator mean the same thing across countries and years?
  2. How are the parts combined, and where do the cut-offs sit?
  3. What validity evidence exists beyond agreement with similar measures?
