Rules of the game

The fine print

25% · Reading quizzes

Six 20-minute quizzes open the class and cover the readings and lectures since the previous quiz. Your best four count, so the two weakest drop automatically.

That drop also absorbs absences. There are no make-ups and no paperwork. Miss more than two and come see me.

25% · Group work

Groups form in week 2 and stay together. Most Thursdays you apply the week's framework to a case or policy problem you have never seen.

Each group hands in a short written output at the end of class, marked on a three-point scale. Your lowest mark drops.

10% · Participation

I grade the quality of your engagement with the reading, on Thursdays and at the complements.

Disagreeing with the author counts. One sharp point about the text beats ten vague ones.

40% · Final exam

Written, in person and cumulative, on Thursday 17 December. You write an essay that reasons with the material, often about a country or policy problem that is new to you.

Missing the exam without a documented justification means a failing grade.

Why compare

Compared with what?

Describe

Is Swiss turnout low? A single number means little until it sits next to other countries.

Explain

Check whether a factor travels with an outcome across cases, while holding other things roughly constant.

Advise

Before recommending a reform, look at what it did in places that already tried it.

For Lijphart, comparison is a method of control. We compare to rule out rival explanations for the relationship we care about.

Lijphart 1971

Four ways to test a claim

Experimental

You assign the treatment yourself. The strongest control, and rarely possible with whole countries.

Statistical

Many cases. You control for rival factors mathematically, through partial correlations.

Comparative

The same logic with too few cases for statistics. Control comes from choosing cases carefully.

Case study

One case, studied in depth. It can generate hypotheses, probe them, or show where a theory fails.

The squares show how much control each method gives over rival explanations.

The core problem

Many variables, small N

Variables that might matter ▶ Cases ▶

Four countries and fourteen candidate explanations. Almost any story fits the data. Lijphart suggests four ways out.

  1. Add cases where you can, across more countries or a longer period
  2. Combine related variables into fewer categories
  3. Pick comparable cases that are alike in many respects and differ in the variables you study
  4. Let theory narrow the list to the few variables that matter most
Unit of analysis

A case of what?

A case is one instance of the thing you want to explain, bounded in space and time.

SwitzerlandCanton of SchaffhausenThe 2023 federal electionA single popular vote

One country can supply one case or hundreds. Your question decides whether the unit is a country, a canton, an election, a vote, or a voter.

Most similar or most different

John Stuart Mill set out both logics in A System of Logic (1843). Lijphart's comparable cases follow the most similar design.

Watch out

Three traps

Selecting on the outcome

Study only the countries that democratized and every factor they share looks like a cause. You also need the cases where it did not happen.

Geddes 1990

Cases that copy each other

Neighbors learn from and imitate one another, so countries are rarely independent observations. This is known as Galton's problem.

Stretched concepts

Carry a concept to too many places and it ends up meaning very little. Sartori calls this conceptual stretching.

Sartori 1970
